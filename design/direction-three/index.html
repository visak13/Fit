Roster

Direction three of three. The structural axis is navigation shape.

Roster is the only one of the three directions where the persistent surface carries a list as well as the destinations. On a laptop it is a permanent list-and-detail application: pick a client on the left, work on them on the right, never lose the list. On a phone the same relationship becomes a push. Its palette is near-achromatic and carries one accent, so colour appears only where it means something.

The navigation surface at laptop width

Roster is the only direction where the two navigation layers are physically adjacent, so it is the only one where they can genuinely collide. Four devices keep them apart, and none of them is decoration. If a reader cannot tell at a glance which column moves them between parts of the application and which moves them within one, this direction has failed at the exact point that makes it worth offering.

The contextual layer lives here, inside the content, and it names records rather than destinations. Every one of these leads somewhere that leads back, so there is nowhere in the application whose only exit is the browser's back button.

Layer one — the narrow column, headed "Go to"

Moves you between parts of the application. The same five destinations, in the same order, with the same words, on both devices. It sits on the page floor, its glyphs are drawn a touch heavier so a near-achromatic column does not read as grey furniture, and a selected destination carries its accent bar on its leading edge — where you came in.

Layer two — the wider pane, headed with the destination's own name

Moves you within the destination you are already in. It is the only pale object in the frame, one step off the floor both columns beside it sit on, so it reads as a thing rather than as more furniture. A selected item carries its accent bar on its trailing edge, pointing into the detail it just filled.

The rule between them is measured, not decorative

Both edges of the list pane are drawn with the interactive border token, which clears three to one against every surface it sits on, at double weight. The faint decorative divider is used inside a pane and never between the two layers, because a boundary that carries meaning is never drawn with the token that was not measured.

The contextual layer is lighter here than in the other two directions

The list pane is itself permanent context, so a breadcrumb appears only when the detail drills a level deeper — from a client into one of their past sessions. It names records, never destinations. A destination in a breadcrumb makes the active state in the column opposite a lie.

The navigation surface at phone width

The same information architecture at a narrower width, not a second design. The destination column becomes the bottom bar carrying the same five destinations in the same order with the same words; the list pane becomes the screen; the detail is pushed over it. Both frames below actually work: press a client, then press the way back.

The synchronisation indicator, in all six states

Permanently visible, unmissable, and it never blocks anything and is never a modal. Five of the six are the time-based ladder. The sixth is not a sixth rung: a change that was refused is different in kind from one that is merely delayed, because a delay resolves itself and a rejection never does. Folded into the ladder it would sit at the floor forever and read as perfectly healthy.

Each state is distinguished by silhouette as well as by colour, and each carries a word. The row beneath each one shows its silhouette in the page's own ink at the rendered size and small — which is what a colour-blind reader, a phone in sunlight, a greyscale screenshot and a compressed video call are left with. Roster is near-achromatic, which makes it the direction where a state carried by colour alone would disappear first.

Backed up 4 minutes ago. Nothing waiting.

Everything on this device has reached the Drive backup. The calm state still says so in words and a time; it never collapses to a bare dot.

Ladder, rung one
Waiting to back up 3 changes waiting. Saved on this device 1 minute ago.

Work is queued and will go on its own. This is the ordinary state during a session and it is deliberately not drawn as a warning: the queue is doing its job.

Ladder, rung two
Not backed up for 7 hours 18 changes waiting. Open the app on a connection when you can.

From six hours. Worth noticing, not worth stopping for. It gains an outline and a bell silhouette; the wording says how long and how many, because a duration is what he can act on.

Ladder, rung three
Not backed up for 2 days 64 changes waiting. This needs a connection today.

From twenty-four hours. It becomes a filled block rather than an outlined one, so it is plainly more serious by shape and not merely by a deeper tint. It recurs on every screen and it still never blocks the application: an app that refuses to open loses the very session it was protecting.

Ladder, rung four
No connection Working from this device. Everything is saving here as usual.

This is not a failure and it is not drawn as one. The application is offline-first: this device holds the primary copy and the Drive is the backup, so working without a connection is the application working. It takes neither the warning nor the danger fill, its wording says what is happening rather than what is missing, and its silhouette is a plain unplugged shape rather than anything broken. Training a client in a basement gym is an ordinary Tuesday, and an indicator that cries wolf there is one he stops reading.

Ladder, rung five — normal operation
One change was refused Tuesday's session for Priya Raghavan. It will not go on its own. Open it to see why.

Not a rung on the ladder above. A refused change has left the queue, so a count of what is waiting reads as healthy precisely because the broken thing is no longer in it. It therefore gets its own path and its own permanent visibility, and it names what was refused, because only a person can resolve it.

Not a rung — a difference of kind

Tooltips, actually open

Written in his words — what the thing does for him, not what it is implemented as. He will be learning this over a video call, which is what makes these worth their space. Every one of them also opens on keyboard focus, because hover-only help does not exist on a phone and does not exist for anyone driving from the keyboard.

Where he asks: why can I not just type it in here?

The application mark

Roster's geometry, reduced to its simplest form: an off-centre rule dividing a narrow band from a wide field, and one accent selection crossing from the band into the field — the selection on the left is what fills the detail on the right. Flat fills only, no gradient and no transparency, because the operating system rasterises this at sizes nobody here controls. The rule and the selection are what survive at sixteen pixels; the list rows are meant to be the first thing to go.

Both themes, side by side

The same token names with different values, switched by one attribute on the root element — never a second stylesheet. Light is near-achromatic on a warm-neutral floor; dark sits on near-black, which on the iPhone the coach actually uses means the unlit pixels of an evening session are close to genuinely off. The accent is a deep teal in light and a pale teal in dark, and both were measured rather than picked.

The trade-off, in one sentence

Roster is the fastest of the three for moving between clients and back again and never loses your place in the list, and the price is a permanently narrower working area on the laptop and one more moving part to learn.

It is not the recommended direction. Ledger is, on the grounds that the risk this application is most likely to fail on is becoming tiring rather than lacking a feature. Roster is the right answer if moving between clients turns out to be the dominant motion of a working day — it is the best of the three at that one thing and the most costly at everything else.
